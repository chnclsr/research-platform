## Core findings

The body of evidence collectively indicates that small modular reactors (SMRs) are being developed across the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and Europe with a shared emphasis on technical standardization and project delivery efficiency, as supported by the NEA SMR Dashboard and industry reviews. SMRs are defined by a capacity of 300 MW or less per unit, in contrast to large-scale reactors that range from 550 to 1,500 MW, suggesting a design shift toward scalability and modular deployment. While SMRs are positioned as offering firm, dispatchable, carbon-free baseload power—distinct from intermittent renewables—this value proposition is underpinned by a significant technical and regulatory challenge: the long-term radiotoxicity of SMR spent fuel, which is projected to be 50% higher than conventional spent fuel after 10,000 years, undermining claims of simplified waste management. This finding contradicts optimistic narratives about SMR environmental and public acceptance benefits. Additionally, despite claims of supply chain standardization and reduced complexity, the global nuclear supply chain has atrophied over three decades of minimal new-build activity, and reconstituting commercial-scale fabrication capacity requires multi-year capital investment that will only emerge after firm orders—creating a chicken-and-egg dilemma. Fuel supply constraints are also evident, with Aalo Atomics’ use of standard 5% low-enriched uranium (LEU) avoiding HALEU bottlenecks, while other designs like TerraPower’s Natrium offer unique dispatchability through integrated energy storage. Cost and performance targets—such as NOAK LCOE ranges of $50–70/MWh—remain aspirational and not yet validated by realized project data. Licensing, financing, and siting are assessed through a consistent framework by the OECD NEA, but no evidence is provided on actual project timelines, cost realizations, or commercial deployment milestones. The absence of data on cancellations, delays, or failed projects, combined with limited independent cost analyses, limits the ability to validate claims of economic viability or project maturity. Overall, while SMRs present a technically distinct value proposition in grid services, their development is constrained by unresolved waste, supply chain fragility, and a lack of demonstrated commercial performance, with public acceptance remaining a significant barrier, particularly in Western nations without operational geological disposal facilities. ## Implementation and practical meaning

The body of evidence collectively indicates that small modular reactor (SMR) projects face significant implementation challenges across cost, supply chain, fuel availability, and regulatory pathways. While SMRs are promoted as solutions to traditional nuclear power’s high capital costs and long timelines—particularly through factory-fabricated, modular designs—empirical data and expert assessments reveal substantial practical limitations. Cost estimates for SMRs range from $4,000 to $7,000 per kW under nominal operating conditions (NOAK), with first-of-a-kind (FOAK) costs projected to be 30–50% higher, reaching $90–120/MWh in levelized cost of electricity (LCOE), which remains above current natural gas prices in liberalized markets without carbon pricing. This cost structure is undermined by the absence of commercial-scale supply chains for nuclear-grade components, a global deficit that creates a chicken-and-egg problem: supply chain development requires firm orders, which in turn depend on successful deployment. A key vulnerability is the dependence on High-Assay Low-Enriched Uranium (HALEU), enriched to 5–20%, where Russia currently dominates the commercial market, introducing severe geopolitical and operational risks. Domestic HALEU enrichment capacity in North America and Europe remains underdeveloped, requiring years of investment and infrastructure development to mitigate this dependency. This fuel bottleneck is cited as a critical constraint on deployment timelines, equal in importance to regulatory hurdles. The cancellation of NuScale’s flagship project—costs rising from $5.3 billion to $9.3 billion—demonstrates the risk of uncontrolled cost escalation and highlights the fragility of SMR financial models. Furthermore, SMRs inherently lose economies of scale compared to large reactors, and their waste streams are more complex and voluminous than conventional reactors, raising environmental and policy concerns. Despite federal policy support in the U.S., including executive orders and funding programs, these initiatives do not resolve underlying structural issues. The evidence from academic and regulatory sources—particularly from researchers like Philseo Kim and Allison Macfarlane—suggests that SMRs are not yet cost-competitive or operationally viable in the near term, and their practical implementation remains constrained by technical, financial, and geopolitical factors. These findings collectively point to a gap between optimistic design claims and real-world deployment realities, indicating that SMRs are not yet a mature or reliable solution for energy supply in Western markets. ## Limitations and risks

The available evidence indicates that small modular reactor (SMR) cost projections remain highly uncertain, with realistic estimates significantly exceeding $100 per MWh. Real-world data from operational or near-operational projects in Russia, China, and the United States suggest that actual costs can be multiples of $10,000 per MWh, with some projects experiencing cost overruns of up to 200% compared to initial estimates. This level of cost escalation challenges the feasibility of SMR deployment under current financial assumptions, particularly when nominal prices are used without adjustment for inflation or real-world performance. The evidence further shows that, despite claims by project developers, only three Western SMR designs are currently in advanced licensing or deployment stages—each based on light-water reactor (LWR) technology—and these represent the most credible near-term pathways. The use of LWRs is strategically significant because it avoids the regulatory uncertainty associated with non-light-water designs, which have historically faced greater delays and higher risk of failure. However, the limited number of real-world projects and the absence of verified, realized cost data from completed SMR deployments in North America or Europe mean that cost and schedule performance remain speculative. No evidence in the packet addresses supply chain resilience, financing mechanisms, or commercial maturity beyond the licensing stage, and no project-specific data on cancellations, delays, or failures is provided. As a result, the body of evidence does not allow for a robust assessment of supply chain or financial risk, nor does it validate company claims about cost efficiency or commercial readiness. The lack of independent, realized cost data from actual deployments—especially in the U.S., Canada, the U.K., or across Europe—undermines the ability to assess whether current projections reflect reality or remain optimistic assumptions. ## Validation and generalisability

The body of evidence indicates that small modular reactor (SMR) projects across the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and Europe from 1 January 2023 through 30 July 2026 do not resolve the fundamental challenges of nuclear energy—such as high capital costs, long construction timelines, regulatory complexity, and supply chain vulnerabilities—instead reproducing these issues in a scaled-down form. Rather than offering a transformative solution, SMRs introduce new complications, including reduced economies of scale, increased project-specific engineering risks, and fragmented supply chains that limit interoperability and standardization. This pattern is consistent across all jurisdictions, with no evidence of a demonstrable improvement in cost efficiency, construction speed, or regulatory streamlining. The claim that SMRs 'replicate' nuclear’s core challenges is supported by the available evidence, which shows that while modular design may offer some flexibility, it does not mitigate the underlying structural and systemic issues of nuclear development. ## Sonuç

Small modular reactors (SMRs) are not yet a commercially viable or operationally proven solution for energy supply in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, or Europe. Despite design claims of cost efficiency, faster deployment, and simplified waste management, the body of evidence reveals that SMRs inherit and exacerbate the core structural challenges of nuclear energy—high capital costs, long construction timelines, regulatory complexity, and supply chain fragility. The projected 50% higher radiotoxicity of SMR spent fuel after 10,000 years undermines environmental and public acceptance, particularly in Western nations lacking geological disposal infrastructure. Cost projections—ranging from $4,000 to $7,000 per kW under NOAK—are not validated by real-world performance, and the absence of realized cost data or inflation-adjusted benchmarks renders direct comparisons between designs or jurisdictions impossible. The global nuclear supply chain remains atrophied, with no evidence of commercial-scale fabrication capacity or supply chain readiness, creating a chicken-and-egg dependency between investment and deployment. The cancellation of NuScale’s project due to cost escalation from $5.3 billion to $9.3 billion exemplifies the fragility of financial models and the risk of uncontrolled cost overruns. Without verified data on construction schedules, financing, or project performance, claims of commercial maturity or economic competitiveness remain speculative. The evidence further indicates that SMRs do not offer a transformative solution to nuclear energy’s systemic challenges, as they replicate rather than resolve issues of cost, scalability, and supply chain resilience. Therefore, SMR deployment in the near term is not supported by demonstrated performance, and significant investment in domestic HALEU enrichment, supply chain reconstitution, and long-term waste management is required before any credible path to commercial viability can be established.
